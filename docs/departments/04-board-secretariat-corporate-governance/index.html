AI use cases in practice

How teams like this actually use AI today, each with its source.

  • Summarising board materials to highlight key information; Nasdaq added Azure OpenAI summarisation to Boardvantage in 2024 [3]
  • Distilling lengthy board papers into clear summaries and supporting agenda planning [1]
  • Drafting board minutes from meetings, with human cross-checking before circulation [1]
  • Administration: summarising meetings, transcripts, notes and board packs, plus regulatory scanning and alerts [2]
  • Drafting routine narrative sections of the annual report, with a warning that generic AI text may not reflect company-specific risks [1]

Capstone deliverable

The one real piece of work a learner produces to pass. It follows the handbook's assignment template.

A mock board pack and minutes set: AI-drafted summary of three board papers, draft minutes from a supplied transcript, and an action register, plus a one-page AI-use policy stating what counts as the official minute, where AI drafts are stored and when they are deleted. Include prompts, a line-by-line accuracy check against the transcript, and time saved. Core-tier hours.

Data risks and rules

The sensitive data this team handles, the law that applies, and the rules that follow.

Board deliberations, MNPI, privileged legal advice and director personal data (GDPR). AI transcripts and summaries raise questions about which document is the official record, retention and later disclosure in litigation; the claim that sharing AI output beyond the core group risks waiving privilege is not verified from an opened page. Governance bodies advise that AI use sit within policies on accountability and content ownership [4]. Rules: define the formal minutes, restrict who receives AI output, and exclude privileged sessions from AI recording.

Adoption KPIs

Numbers to track so you know whether the training changed anything.

Hours to produce a board pack; days from meeting to approved minutes; number of corrections found in AI-drafted minutes; share of secretariat staff trained and following the AI-use policy.

Curriculum emphasis

Where the hours should go, and which tools to prioritise.

Prioritise Microsoft Teams Copilot and Microsoft 365 Copilot (meeting capture and drafting), Adobe Acrobat AI Assistant (searching PDF board packs; not verified by an opened source) and Claude for long-paper summaries. Most time should go to record-keeping rules and verification, because the risk lies in minutes and privilege, not drafting speed.

Tool-list fit

Whether the handbook's tool list suits this team, and what it misses.

The list fits, but omits dedicated board-portal AI (for example Nasdaq Boardvantage) [3], which is where many boards already keep their papers. NotebookLM is useful for document search but its enterprise data terms should be checked before board material goes in (not verified). Gemini and Claude are general tools and need secure-tenant approval.

Jargon on this page

  • AI assistantA tool you talk to in plain language (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot). It drafts, summarises and answers, but it can be wrong, so you check its work.
  • PromptThe instruction or question you give an AI tool. Clear prompts with context and a desired format get better results.
  • TierThe handbook's training depth: Core (6-10 hours per tool), Advanced (12-20), Specialist (20-40+). A department's tier sets how long to budget.
  • VerifiedVerified means a researcher opened the page and read the claim on it. Not verified means it came from a search snippet or a page that would not load, so treat it with care.
  • RetentionHow long a tool keeps your prompts and outputs. Enterprise plans usually let an administrator set this.
  • CopilotMicrosoft's name for its AI assistants inside Word, Excel, Teams, Power BI, security tools and Azure. One licence covers several of them.
  • GDPRThe EU's data protection law. Any AI use involving information about identifiable people has to respect it.
