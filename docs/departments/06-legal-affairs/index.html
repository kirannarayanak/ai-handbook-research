AI use cases in practice

How teams like this actually use AI today, each with its source.

  • Lease and contract summaries: extraction templates run across a contract folder, then attorney review, to find critical terms and deadlines [1]
  • Litigation support: locating operative clauses, building chronologies from correspondence, spotting notice defects, turning a day-long first pass into a focused review [1]
  • Deposition transcript analysis with page-line citations and inconsistency flags, treated as draft work product [1]
  • Tabular reviews of contracts and production sets as a more advanced stage once workflows are tested (not individually verified) [1]
  • Starting with lower-risk repetitive tasks (summaries, first-pass review) under an AI-use policy with mandatory attorney review [1]

Capstone deliverable

The one real piece of work a learner produces to pass. It follows the handbook's assignment template.

A clause-comparison and contract-summary pack for five sample contracts against a playbook: extraction table, deviations list, suggested redlines, with every citation checked against the source, a hallucination log, and an AI-use policy page covering privilege and confidentiality. Include prompts and time saved against manual review. Specialist-tier hours.

Data risks and rules

The sensitive data this team handles, the law that applies, and the rules that follow.

Attorney-client privilege and work product, confidential client and company information, personal data (GDPR) and trade secrets. In an ACC global survey of in-house counsel, 56% believed AI tools could compromise privilege and generative AI was seen as the highest-risk category (49%) [2]. Rules: approved enterprise tools with contractual limits on data use, retention and training; review vendor terms (ABA Formal Opinion 512, 2024) [1]; never rely on AI legal research without verification, because of hallucinated citations [1]; obtain consent before putting client data into insufficiently protected tools [1].

Adoption KPIs

Numbers to track so you know whether the training changed anything.

Contract review turnaround time; share of AI outputs with all citations verified; number of privilege or confidentiality incidents (target zero); share of matters using the approved workflow.

Curriculum emphasis

Where the hours should go, and which tools to prioritise.

Prioritise Thomson Reuters CoCounsel (legal research with citations; not verified by an opened source), Microsoft Word Copilot (redlines in the document where lawyers work), Claude (long-contract analysis) and NotebookLM (source-grounded Q&A). Most Specialist hours go to verification, privilege rules and policy design.

Tool-list fit

Whether the handbook's tool list suits this team, and what it misses.

Good fit. Word Copilot is narrow but sensible for drafting. Gemini is generic and its enterprise terms need checking. The handbook omits dedicated contract-AI and legal AI platforms such as Harvey, described in a vendor source for diligence (see department 03 source); no opened legal-department source ranks tools, so treat as not verified.

Jargon on this page

  • PromptThe instruction or question you give an AI tool. Clear prompts with context and a desired format get better results.
  • HallucinationWhen an AI states something false with confidence, including invented quotes, numbers or links. The fix is to open the source before you repeat it.
  • TierThe handbook's training depth: Core (6-10 hours per tool), Advanced (12-20), Specialist (20-40+). A department's tier sets how long to budget.
  • VerifiedVerified means a researcher opened the page and read the claim on it. Not verified means it came from a search snippet or a page that would not load, so treat it with care.
  • RetentionHow long a tool keeps your prompts and outputs. Enterprise plans usually let an administrator set this.
  • CopilotMicrosoft's name for its AI assistants inside Word, Excel, Teams, Power BI, security tools and Azure. One licence covers several of them.
  • GDPRThe EU's data protection law. Any AI use involving information about identifiable people has to respect it.
